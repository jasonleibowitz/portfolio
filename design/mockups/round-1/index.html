Three design directions for leibowitz.me

Static mockups for issue #7. Each direction is one self-contained HTML file with four screens — home, blog index, single post, and a design spec — in light and dark. Nothing here is production code; the point is to pick a direction. Every direction is mobile-first: the default view below is 360px, and the nav strategy is different in each.

Recommendation. Start from A and graft two things from B: its blog index (the monospace date column beats a paragraph list once there are more than ten posts) and its <details> menu if the nav ever grows past five items. A is the direction that most flatters four long, well-written posts and one 243-line illustrated espresso guide; it is also the cheapest to keep looking good as content is added. C is the one to pick only if the goal is to be remembered by a design- adjacent audience — it costs the most and will need revisiting in two years.

The nav decision (the structural one)

The current header needs 345px of content at a 320px viewport, which is the single cause of the overflow on every page. Three ways out, one per direction:

Strategy Pros Cons
A — stack & wrap No JS, no state, no focus management, nothing to get wrong. Every destination stays visible and one tap away. Degrades to a single row at ≥736px with no duplicate markup. Costs a second header line on phones. Stops scaling past ~5 links — a sixth would wrap to a third row.
B — <details> disclosure Conventional and compact; header stays 56px and can be sticky. Native element means keyboard, screen-reader and no-JS behaviour are free — no focus trap to implement wrong. Scales to any number of links. Hides destinations behind a tap; measurably lower engagement with secondary pages. Needs ~10 lines of JS if you want Escape-to-close and close-on-outside-click.
C — bottom tab bar Thumb-reachable, always visible, no hidden state, tap targets are large by construction. Feels deliberate rather than defaulted. Permanently spends ~62px of a phone screen. Reads as "app", which is a strange frame for a personal blog. Interacts badly with iOS Safari's collapsing toolbar; needs env(safe-area-inset-bottom) and careful testing.

What each direction does with the espresso post (#12)

That post is 243 lines with nine CaptionedImage calls, and it is the most substantial thing on the site. It is the right stress test for the article template.

  • A — images break out past the 34rem measure on desktop and sit full-width on mobile; captions are small sans under a warm hairline. A drop cap and a pull quote give a long read some texture. Best of the three for a 2,400-word piece.
  • B — images are contained inside the measure with a 1px border and fig. n captions, plus a table of contents block at the top. Reads like documentation. Excellent for the how-to posts, slightly austere for the espresso one.
  • C — a cobalt hero band with the cover image pulled up into it, full-bleed figures and an acid pull quote. The most impressive single screenshot; also the most likely to fight with photos that have their own strong colour.

All three assume one fix to the content itself: the post's body starts at # (h1), so a published page currently has three h1s. Whichever direction wins, body headings shift down one level.

Dark mode — resolve it, don't leave it half-built

html.dark exists in global.css and nothing ever sets it. All three directions propose the same fix rather than three different ones, because there is only one sane implementation:

  • Set data-theme on <html> from a small blocking script in <head> — localStorage first, then prefers-color-scheme. Blocking is the point; deferring it produces a white flash on every navigation of a static site.
  • Replace the dead html.dark block with @custom-variant dark (&:where([data-theme=dark] *)) in global.css, so Tailwind's dark: utilities drive off the same attribute.
  • Where the toggle lives is the only real difference: A puts it in the footer (quiet), B and C put it in the header (persistent).

The alternative — deleting the dead styles and shipping light-only — is defensible for a four-post blog, but a developer portfolio without dark mode gets noticed by exactly the audience you are aiming at. Cost is about half a day.

Footer (#11)

Footer.astro is orphaned, and its fixed h-40 plus purple→blue gradient would not survive being rendered anyway. All three directions render a footer and delete that component:

  • A — a quiet band on the warm surface tone: links, colophon, theme toggle. Stacks on mobile.
  • B — a single hairline row, 12px mono, copyright and five links.
  • C — a full-width ink block with a large "let's build something" line and contact links; doubles as the call to action a job-search site should have.

What each direction deletes

Same list for all three, which is worth knowing before the audits run:

  • The two unrelated gradients (orange→rose logo, purple→blue nav hover) — gone in every direction.
  • HeadshotSocialLinks, the only React island. The hover-swap headshot is a fun trick, but it costs React on the homepage for an effect nobody sees on a phone. A and C keep a static portrait; B drops the headshot entirely. This means the site ships zero JS islands — @astrojs/react stays installed only if something else needs it.
  • The five 48px social icon PNGs on the homepage → text links (A, B) or two buttons plus a footer row (C).
  • float-right on the About page, and every fixed w-64/h-40.
  • The bare prose defaults, replaced by an explicit article style in each direction.

Honest weaknesses

  • A — the least distinctive at a glance. A serif on warm paper is the house style of a thousand good blogs; it will not make anyone say "who made this". The wrap-nav also caps out at about five destinations.
  • B — near-monochrome can read as unfinished to a non-technical viewer, and with only five posts a dense index looks emptier than a generous list does. It is the strongest of the three at 50 posts and the weakest at 5.
  • C — the most work, the most to maintain, and the first to look dated. The colour system also constrains the writing: cobalt and coral cards fight photographs, so image-led posts need care. White-on-cobalt is 5.9:1, which is fine for headings but rules out body copy on the coloured blocks.

How this was verified

Screenshots hide 10–20px of overflow, so each screen of each direction was measured in Chrome by comparing document.documentElement.scrollWidth against the viewport width at 320, 360, 390, 414 and 768px, in both themes. Results are in MEASUREMENTS.md next to these files; the row under each frame above shows the live number for whatever you have selected.
