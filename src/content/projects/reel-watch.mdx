## The problem

There is always more to watch than anyone could get through. The problem is choosing, and on your own that is twenty minutes of scrolling. With other people it is even more scrolling plus a negotiation. The apps meant to help share three flaws, and Reel is built around all three.

1. **Rating in the abstract does not work.** Is Forrest Gump a 4.5 or a 5? Whatever I pick is arbitrary, and I would not be able to defend it a year later. Ask whether I liked it more than Finding Nemo and the answer is immediate, and it is still true a year from now.
2. **A new app knows nothing about what you have already seen.** There is no realistic way to enter a thousand films you have watched, so it spends its first months recommending things you saw years ago.
3. **A recommendation you cannot act on is not one.** Being told to watch something excellent on a service you do not subscribe to just moves the problem along.

## What it does

Reel asks you to compare, not to score. It puts the film you just watched beside one you have already ranked and asks which you liked more. A few answers is all it takes, and the order that comes out is one you still agree with a year later.

Nobody starts from nothing, so you can also log films you have already seen without ranking each one, which turns a back catalog of hundreds into a single sitting. What comes out is your library: what you have watched, what you mean to watch, your own notes, and a film page carrying Rotten Tomatoes, IMDb, TMDB and Metacritic alongside your score.

From there Reel learns what you actually like, and every recommendation tells you where the film is streaming, so it reads "watch this, it is on your Netflix" rather than sending you to a service you do not pay for.

Reel is a single-player app first, because a recommendation engine that does not know you is worth nothing to your friends either. Following people, matching taste between libraries, and choosing something for a room rather than a person are what comes after.

## How it's built

Ranking a film starts with one tap on its poster: _Liked It_ or _Not For Me_. After that it is a binary search. Every head-to-head comparison halves the range the film could sit in, so slotting it into a library of hundreds takes a handful of questions rather than a walk down the list, and it stays a handful as the library grows.

The score falls out of where it lands. Each of the two answers owns a slice of the ten-point scale, and a film's position inside its slice interpolates a number within it, so a film you said you liked never prints in the 4s. That matters more than it sounds. People read a 4 as a bad film, because a 4 is a bad film on IMDb and Letterboxd, and a scale that argues with the ones already in someone's head loses. No score is ever stored either. It is computed from position each time it is read, which is why scores shift a little as a library fills out, and why a 9.1 in Reel says something about your taste rather than borrowing a scale everyone reads differently.

Behind that, a Django API and an Expo app in one monorepo, with the mobile client's types generated from the API's own schema, so the two halves cannot drift apart without the build saying so. Film data comes from TMDB and OMDB, kept current by background jobs and insulated well enough that a slow provider costs a detail rather than the whole screen.
